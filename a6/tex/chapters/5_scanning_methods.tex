\section{Scanning Methods}

An x-ray measurement always consists of an intensity measurement as a function of
the goniometer movement. The intensity profile ideally consists of several sharp
peaks above a background. The width of the peaks is determined by the crystal quality
of the sample and is therefore of particular interest.

The goniometer movement changes the scattering vector $\mathbf{q}$ as well as the
relative position of the laboratory coordinate system with respect to the reciprocal
crystal lattice. Different constraints on the goniometer motion lead to a
variety of scanning methods. Each has a different trajectory through the Ewald Sphere
and therefore reveals different information about the sample. Scanning methods
used in this laboratory course are: $2\theta\text{-}\omega$-scans, $\omega$-scans
and reciprocal space maps. Additionally, the sample miscut can be calculated by
a special sequence of $\omega$-scans. This section is based on findings of \autocite{wsi2007hrxrd}

\subsection{Miscut Identification}
\label{sec:miscut}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{../assets/scan_miscut.png}
	\caption{Visualization of a miscut that tilts the reciprocal-lattice vector
	$\mathbf{G}$ by the angle $\gamma$ out of the $\hat{q}_{\perp}$ direction. Rotating
	the sample about $\varphi$ sweeps the projection onto the scattering plane.}
	\label{fig:scan_miscut}
\end{figure}

An x-ray diffractometer offers a very high resolution in the scattering plane, but
a lower resolution perpendicular to it. The axial acceptance describes the
angular range, out of the scattering plane, over which the diffractometer can
still detect a reflection. For those reflexes, the diffractometer effectively
measures the projection onto the scattering plane.

A symmetrical peak with miscut, depicted in \cref{fig:scan_miscut}, can be described
with respect to the laboratory coordinate system as:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{G}=\lvert \mathbf{G}\rvert
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\sin(\gamma) \cos(\varphi+\varphi_{0}) \\
		\sin(\gamma) \sin(\varphi+\varphi_{0}) \\
		\cos(\gamma)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The first component corresponds to $q_{\parallel}$, the third component to
$q_{\perp}$. The second component describes the out of scattering plane amount.

A perfectly aligned crystal features $\gamma = 0$. Since the miscut is small, the
reflex lies within the axial acceptance for all $\varphi$. Therefore, the projection
is measured as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		q_{\parallel} & =\lvert \mathbf{G}\rvert \sin(\gamma) \cos(\varphi+\varphi_{0}) \\
		q_{\perp}     & =\lvert \mathbf{G}\rvert \cos(\gamma).
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Since the miscut is small, the length change of the projection is negligible.
Therefore, all reflexes are observed for a fixed $\theta_{\mathrm{B}}$. Using
\cref{eq:coordinate_transformation} a relation $\omega(\varphi)$ can be
established to fit the data with $\varphi_{0}$ and $\gamma$ as parameters:
\begin{equation}
	\omega-\theta_{\mathrm{B}}=\arctan\left( \frac{q_{\parallel}}{q_{\perp}}\right)
	=\arctan[\tan(\gamma)\cos(\varphi+\varphi_{0})].
\end{equation}
The $\omega(\varphi)$ data are obtained by performing an $\omega$-scan at the fixed
Bragg angle $\theta_{\mathrm{B}}$ for each $\varphi$ angle and recording the $\omega$
position of maximum intensity.

\citeauthoryear{sass_x-ray_2006} define the following helper angles
$\gamma_{0,180}$ and $\gamma_{90,270}$, which are related to $\gamma$ as:
\begin{align}
	\gamma_{0, 180}  & =\frac{1}{2}[(\omega_{0}-\theta_{\mathrm{B}})-(\omega_{180}-\theta_{\mathrm{B}})]=\arctan[\tan(\gamma) \cos(\varphi_{0})]    \\
	\gamma_{90, 270} & = \frac{1}{2}[(\omega_{270}-\theta_{\mathrm{B}})-(\omega_{90}-\theta_{\mathrm{B}})]=\arctan[\tan(\gamma) \sin(\varphi_{0})].
\end{align}
This yields the following identity: $\tan(\gamma_{0, 180})^{2}+\tan(\gamma_{90, 270}
)^{2}=\tan^{2}(\gamma)$. Using the small-angle approximation results in
$\gamma^{2}=\gamma_{0, 180}^{2}+\gamma_{90, 270}^{2}$.

\subsection{$2\theta\text{-}\omega$-Scans}
\label{sec:2to}

In a $2\theta\text{-}\omega$-scan, the sample is rotated around the $\omega$-axis
while the detector rotates with twice the angular velocity, $\chi$ and $\varphi$ angles
are kept constant. This routine only changes the magnitude of the scattering
vector $\mathbf{q}$. Its direction remains constant, since the scattering vector
is via \cref{eq:coordinate_transformation}:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{q}= 2k \sin(\theta)
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\sin(\omega-\theta) \\
		\cos(\omega-\theta)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Symmetrical reflexes are reflexes that can be described by a reciprocal space vector
parallel to $\hat{q}_{\perp}$. By setting $\omega=\theta$, these reflexes can be observed.

Measuring an intensity maximum in a $2\theta\text{-}\omega$-scan therefore yields
the magnitude of the scattering vector $\mathbf{q}$. Given a guess about which
lattice plane the reflex corresponds to, this magnitude allows conclusions about the
lattice constants (see \cref{sec:reciprocal_space}). This is illustrated in
\cref{fig:scan_2to}, which shows the trajectory through reciprocal space. Due to the
finite layer thickness, the peak broadens in the $\hat{q}_{\perp}$ direction
(\cref{fig:scan_2to_error}).

\begin{figure}

		\caption{Trajectories of $2\theta\text{-}\omega$ and $\omega$ scans. Image taken
		from \autocite{wsi2007hrxrd}.}
		\label{fig:scan_2to}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.35\linewidth}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../assets/scan_2to_error.png}
		\caption{Peak broadening in $\hat{q}_{\perp}$ direction, originating from finite

		\label{fig:scan_2to_error}
	\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

Since thin films exhibit columnar growth, the vertical coherence length is not infinite
but limited by the layer thickness, which is in order of
$\qty{100}{\nano\meter}$. The delta-distribution approximation in the $z$-direction
of the intensity integral in \cref{eq:periodic_intensity} does not hold. Instead,
the reflex acquires the finite width of the $\mathrm{sinc}$ function and broadens
in the $\hat{q}_{\perp}$ direction:
\begin{equation}
	I(\mathbf{q}) \propto \left| \int_{V}\exp(\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{q})\cdot
	\mathbf{r}) \, \mathrm{d}V(\mathbf{r}) \right|^{2}\simeq l_{x}l_{y}\delta(q_{x}
	, G_{x}) \delta(q_{y}, G_{y}) \left| \frac{\sin\left( l_{z}\frac{1}{2}(G_{z}-q_{z})
	\right)}{\frac{1}{2}(G_{z}-q_{z})}\right|^{2}.
\end{equation}
The FWHM of the reflex intensity correlates with the layer thickness. If other broadening
effects are neglected, the thickness can be determined by:
\begin{equation}
	l_{z}=\frac{0.9 \lambda}{\Delta(2\theta) \cos(\theta)}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{$\omega$-Scans}

\begin{figure}
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.6\linewidth}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../assets/scan_omega_error.png}
		\caption{Peak broadening in a mosaic crystal due to (a) finite coherence
		length (b) tilt of crystallites. Image taken from \autocite{wsi2007hrxrd}.}
		\label{fig:scan_omega_error}
	\end{minipage}
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.3\linewidth}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../assets/scan_omega_mosaic.png}
		\caption{Diffraction for a mosaic crystal. Image taken from \autocite{wsi2007hrxrd}.}
		\label{fig:scan_omega_mosaicity}
	\end{minipage}
	\label{fig:scan_omega_full}
\end{figure}

In an $\omega$-scan, the sample is rotated around the $\omega$-axis, while
$\theta$, $\varphi$ and $\chi$ are kept constant. This routine moves the
scattering vector on a circular path around the origin, explained by \cref{eq:coordinate_transformation}.
The peak broadening mechanisms are illustrated in \cref{fig:scan_omega_error} and
\cref{fig:scan_omega_mosaicity}:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{q}= 2k \sin(\theta)
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\sin(\omega-\theta) \\
		\cos(\omega-\theta)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

Due to a columnar growth mode, the lateral planes perpendicular to the growth
direction have a finite width (coherence length) for scattering, limited by the crystallite
size. Similar to the discussion in \cref{sec:2to}, this leads to a peak broadening
in $q_{\mathrm{\parallel}}$ direction. For a symmetrical rocking curve, assuming that
the limited coherence size is the only reason for peak broadening, the coherence
length can be estimated as:
\begin{equation}
	L_{\parallel}=\frac{0.9 \lambda}{2\Delta\omega\cdot \sin(\theta)}.\label{eq:coherence_length}
\end{equation}

Another broadening mechanism originates from the tilt of the crystallites. A mosaic
crystal is built up by many crystallites with tilt angles $\delta\omega$. These
crystallites can be grouped into ensembles: Rotating the sample around the $\omega$-axis
selects different ensembles. The width of a symmetrical rocking curve is
therefore influenced by a finite crystal size and the tilt of crystallites. The FWHM
values of these peaks are becomes a figure of merit. The smaller the better the
crystallites are aligned and the larger their size.

The crystallite misorientation has two independent components: a tilt about axes lying
in the surface plane and a twist about the growth direction $\hat{q}_{\perp}$. A
symmetric reflection has its scattering vector parallel to $\hat{q}_{\perp}$, so a
twist about this axis leaves the reflection invariant. Its rocking-curve width
$\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{tilt}}$ therefore captures the tilt alone. Accessing the twist
requires a reflection whose scattering vector has a component in the surface plane.
The twist width $\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{twist}}$ is obtained in the limit of a
purely in-plane reflection, which cannot be reached in coplanar geometry and must
instead be extrapolated from a set of asymmetric reflections recorded in skew geometry
(or approximated by a single asymmetric reflection with a strong in-plane
component).

Assuming that the rocking-curve broadening is caused solely by tilt and twist,
the dislocation densities can be estimated with respect to the growth direction
from the two widths:
\begin{align}
	\rho_{\mathrm{screw}} & = \frac{\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{tilt}}^{2}}{4.35 \lvert \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{screw}}\rvert^{2}}\label{eq:rho_screw} \\
	\rho_{\mathrm{edge}}  & = \frac{\Delta\omega_{\mathrm{twist}}^{2}}{4.35 \lvert \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{edge}}\rvert^{2}}.\label{eq:rho_edge}
\end{align}
Here $\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{screw}}$ is the Burgers vector of the screw-type dislocations,
aligned with the growth direction and dominating the tilt, while $\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{edge}}$
is that of the edge-type dislocations, lying in the surface plane and dominating
the twist.

\subsection{Reciprocal Space Maps}

A \ac{rsm} scans a two-dimensional region of reciprocal space. The scan can be carried
out by repeating an $\omega$-scan for a defined range followed by a tiny shift using
the $2\theta\text{-}\omega$ scan mode to change the length of the scattering vector.
The angles can be related to $q_{\parallel}$ and $q_{\perp}$ components via
\cref{eq:coordinate_transformation}. The map is therefore usually plotted in $q$ coordinates
rather than the raw goniometer angles, which provides a direct visualization of
the measured region of reciprocal space and the underlying Ewald sphere
construction. Broadening due to finite coherence length, mosaicity and finite
layer thickness is visible.
