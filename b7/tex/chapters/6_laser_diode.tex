\section{Laser Diode}\label{sec:laser_diode}


\subsection{Vertical Structure}\label{sec:vertical_structure}

% Gliederung: [[Double Heterostructure]]: Ladungsträger- und Lichteinschluss, Leckstrom $I_{\mathrm L}$
% ---- Double Heterostructure.md ----

A thin layer of low-band-gap material is embedded between two layers of higher-band-gap material.
The band-gap step confines charge carriers and the refractive-index step confines light, both in the same thin layer. A popular material system is $\ce{GaAs}$ as gain medium with $\ce{AlGaAs}$ as cladding.

The band offsets $\Delta E_{\mathrm{C}}$ and $\Delta E_{\mathrm{V}}$ confine electrons

$F_{\mathrm{P}}$ are separated by $qV$.}
\label{fig:double_heterostructure}

\subsubsection{Electrical Consequences}

The band offsets form potential wells that trap injected charge carriers in the low-gap layer, where they recombine. However, carriers with an energy higher than the barrier height can pass over the barrier, escape from the well and no longer recombine in the active layer. This effect results in a leakage current $I_{\mathrm{L}}$.

\subsubsection{Optical Consequences}

In the transparent regime, $\epsilon'' \simeq 0$ and $n_{\mathrm{r}} \simeq \sqrt{ \epsilon'}$. Using the oscillator model:
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon'=1 + \int \frac{e^{2}}{\epsilon_{0} m_{0} \omega_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}} \frac{2 \lvert \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{cv}} \rvert ^{2}}{m_{0} \hbar \omega_{\mathrm{cv}}} \frac{1}{1-\omega^{2} /\omega_{\mathrm{cv}}^{2}} \, \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}
\end{equation}

If the band-gap energy decreases, $\omega_{\mathrm{cv}}$ decreases (with $\omega < \omega_{\mathrm{cv}}$ and $\lvert \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{cv}} \rvert$ approximately constant), so that all factors of the integrand increase. Hence, both $\epsilon'$ and $n_{\mathrm{r}}$ increase. A low-gap layer consequently has a higher refractive index than its surroundings, leading to wave guiding in this layer. The layer acts like the core of a glass fiber in which light is guided. A key metric is the Optical Confinement Factor.

The optical confinement factor $\Gamma$ is the fraction of the light intensity, integrated over the cross section, that lies within a specified region. For a one-dimensional problem in which the specified region is centered at the origin and has the width $d$, $\Gamma$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\Gamma=\frac{\int_{-d /2}^{d /2} \lvert E(x) \rvert ^{2} \, \mathrm{d}x}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \lvert E(x) \rvert^{2} \, \mathrm{d}x}
\end{equation}

The confinement factor of the gain region is used as a figure of merit in laser diodes. A value of $\Gamma$ close to one is desirable. The layer structure strongly influences $\Gamma$. A Double Heterostructure has a much higher confinement factor than a homojunction.

\subsection{Lateral Structure}\label{sec:lateral_structure}

% Gliederung: [[Laser Diode]]: Abschnitte zur Schichtstruktur und lateralen Struktur (Gain Guiding, Index Guiding)
% ---- Laser Diode.md ----

\subsubsection{Layer Structure}

The earliest semiconductor lasers were homojunction lasers, which were demonstrated in 1962. These “proof of concept” lasers featured a threshold current density of $\SI{19000}{A/cm^{2}}$ at cryogenic temperature. A prominent vertical layer structure that was realized in 1969–1970 is the Double Heterostructure. In comparison to homojunctions, a double heterostructure (DHS) featured a significantly lower threshold current density ($\SI{1600}{A/cm^{2}}$) and could be operated at room temperature.

However, almost all commercial laser diodes today are no longer simple double-heterostructure lasers but quantum-well lasers, usually with a separate-confinement heterostructure (SCH). The first single and multiple quantum-well lasers were demonstrated in 1977 by Holonyak’s group using liquid-phase epitaxy. Confining the electron-hole pairs to the thin well region reduced the threshold current density to about $\SI{500}{A/cm^{2}}$. Introducing strain into the quantum wells in the late 1980s lowered it further to $\SI{65}{A/cm^{2}}$ at room temperature.

\subsection{Resonator and Threshold}\label{sec:resonator_and_threshold}

% Gliederung: [[Fabry-Perot Resonator]]
% ---- Fabry-Perot Resonator.md ----

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering

A crucial part of a laser is a resonator that feeds part of the light back into the amplifying medium. This resonator consists of two plane-parallel mirrors. In semiconductor lasers, the mirrors are realized as smooth cleaved facets at both ends of the active stripe. A laser oscillates when a light wave returns after one round trip with identical amplitude and phase. The gain must then exactly compensate all losses.

\subsubsection{Round-Trip Condition}

The light intensity of the guided mode grows with the modal gain $\Gamma g$ and decays with the intrinsic loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}$. As the electric field amplitude scales with the square root of the intensity, it varies along the cavity as:
\begin{equation}
	E(z)=E_0\exp\left[\tfrac{1}{2}(\Gamma g-\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})z\right]\exp(\mathrm{i}\beta z),\qquad \beta=\frac{2\pi\bar n}{\lambda}
\end{equation}

The intrinsic loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}$ originates from free-carrier absorption in the waveguide materials and from scattering at rough waveguide walls. At each facet only the fraction $r_1$ or $r_2$ of the field amplitude is reflected; after a round trip (length $2L$, two reflections) the field has to reproduce itself:
\begin{equation}
	r_1 r_2\,\exp\left[(\Gamma g-\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})L\right]\exp(2\mathrm{i}\beta L)=1
\end{equation}

As this condition is complex-valued, it splits into one condition for the magnitude (threshold) and one for the phase (modes).

\subsubsection{Amplitude Condition: Threshold}

Since the reflectivities $R_{1,2}$ refer to intensities, the amplitude reflection coefficients are their square roots, $r_1r_2=\sqrt{R_1R_2}$. The magnitude of the round-trip condition fixes the gain needed at threshold:
\begin{equation}
	\sqrt{R_1R_2}\,\exp\left[(\Gamma g_{\mathrm{th}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{i}})L\right]=1
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad
	\Gamma g_{\mathrm{th}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2L}\ln\frac{1}{R_1R_2}}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}
\end{equation}

The mirror loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}$ accounts for the light leaving through the facets, which forms the useful laser output. At threshold, the modal gain balances the intrinsic and mirror losses.

\subsubsection{Phase Condition: Longitudinal Modes}

The phase of the round-trip condition has to be a multiple of $2\pi$:
\begin{equation}
	2\beta L=2\pi m\quad\Rightarrow\quad m\lambda=2L\bar n
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Facet Reflectivity}

The cleaved facets act as mirrors through Fresnel reflection at the semiconductor–air interface; at normal incidence, the reflectivity is:
\begin{equation}
	R=\left(\frac{\bar n-1}{\bar n+1}\right)^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{P-I Characteristic}\label{sec:p_i_characteristic}

% Gliederung: [[Laser Diode P-I Curve]]: verwendet $\Gamma$, $\alpha_{\mathrm m}$, $g_{\mathrm{th}}$ und $I_{\mathrm L}$ aus den vorherigen Abschnitten
% ---- Laser Diode P-I Curve.md ----

A rate equation, similar to the Electron-Hole Continuity Equation, can be used to describe the dynamics of the carrier density $n$ and the Photon Density $S$:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\mathrm{d}n(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}= \eta_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{j(t)}{qd} - R(n) - v_{\mathrm{g}} g(n) S(t)
\end{equation}

% $d$ & thickness of the active region & $\mathrm{m}$ \\
% $R(n)$ & recombination rate per volume & $\mathrm{m^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$ \\

% $S$ & photon density & $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ \\
% \bottomrule

The first term describes the carrier injection into the active region with thickness $d$ and collection efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{i}}$. $R(n)$ represents (radiative and nonradiative) recombination processes. The last term expresses the carrier loss due to Stimulated Emission.

Using the ABC Model, the recombination rate is given as:
\begin{align}
	R(n)&=An +Bn^{2}+Cn^{3}=\frac{n}{\tau(n)} \\
	\tau(n)&=(A+Bn+Cn^{2})^{-1}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Steady State with Low Current Density}

In steady state and at low current density, the carrier concentration is determined by the injection current. As the photon density is negligible ($S \simeq 0$), it follows that:
\begin{equation}
	\eta_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{j}{qd}=\frac{n}{\tau}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Threshold Condition}

The round-trip condition that is enforced in a Fabry-Perot Resonator results in a threshold condition of the material gain:
\begin{equation}
	\Gamma g_{\mathrm{th}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}
\end{equation}

% \begin{table}[H]
% \centering
% \begin{tabular}{lll}
% \toprule
% Symbol & Meaning & Unit \\
% \midrule
% $\Gamma$ & optical confinement factor & dimensionless \\
% $g_{\mathrm{th}}$ & gain coefficient at threshold & $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ \\
% $\alpha_{\mathrm i}$ & intrinsic loss & $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ \\
% $\alpha_{\mathrm m}$ & mirror loss & $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ \\
% \bottomrule
% \end{tabular}
% \caption{TODO}

In this equation, $\Gamma$ is the Optical Confinement Factor. The modal gain $\Gamma g_{\mathrm{th}}$ must equal the sum of the intrinsic loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}$ and the mirror loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}$. With increasing $j$, $n$ increases and therefore $g$ increases up to its threshold value. Consequently, $g_{\mathrm{th}}$ limits $n$ to $n_{\mathrm{th}}$, which is reached for $j=j_{\mathrm{th}}$.
\begin{align}
	g_{\mathrm{th}}&=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Gamma}  \\
	n_{\mathrm{th}}&=n_{\mathrm{tr}} +(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}) /(\Gamma g')  \\
	j_{\mathrm{th}}&=\frac{qd}{\eta_{\mathrm{i}}} (An_{\mathrm{th}}+Bn_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}+Cn_{\mathrm{th}}^{3})
\end{align}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

In this equation, $wLd_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the effective volume of the optical mode, so $S\,(wLd_{\mathrm{op}})$ is the number of photons in the mode, and $v_{\mathrm{g}} \alpha_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the escape rate per photon. Substituting $S$, using $I=jwL$, $I_{\mathrm{th}}=j_{\mathrm{th}}wL$, $g_{\mathrm{th}}=(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}})/\Gamma$ and the confinement factor $\Gamma=d/d_{\mathrm{op}}$ (ratio of active-region thickness to optical-mode thickness), yields the well-known $P_{\mathrm{out}}(I)$ curve:
\begin{equation}
	P_{\mathrm{out}}=\underbrace{ \eta_{\mathrm{i}}  \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}} }_{ \eta_{\mathrm{d}} } \frac{\hbar \omega}{q} (I-I_{\mathrm{th}})
\end{equation}

The slope of the normalized curve $P_{\mathrm{out}} /(\hbar \omega)$ versus $I /q$ is the external differential quantum efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{d}}$. In contrast, the wall-plug efficiency is defined as $P_{\mathrm{out}} / (VI)$ with the applied voltage $V$.

\subsubsection{Real Devices}

\paragraph{Leakage Current}

In real devices, a leakage current exists due to the finite barrier height in the Double Heterostructure. The model can be extended by splitting the total current into the current injected into the active region $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ and the leakage current $I_{\mathrm{L}}$. If $I_{\mathrm{th}}$ additionally includes the threshold leakage current, $P_{\mathrm{out}}$ can be described as
\begin{equation}
	P_{\mathrm{out}}=\eta_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\hbar \omega}{q} \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}} (I-I_{\mathrm{th}}-\Delta I_{\mathrm{L}})
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Line Shape}

Up to the threshold current, only a slight increase in optical power is measured. In this regime, the diode effectively operates as an LED. For currents above the threshold current, the (steep) linear relationship is established, as discussed above. For high currents, the output power saturates. Possible causes are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item An increased leakage current $\Delta I_{\mathrm{L}}(I)$
	\item A current dependence of the threshold current due to junction heating
	\item A current dependence of the intrinsic loss $\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}$
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Temperature Dependence}

The threshold current density is often expressed as a function of temperature, using an empirical formula with the characteristic temperature $T_{0}$ over a temperature range near $T_{1}$:
\begin{equation}
	j_{\mathrm{th}}(T)=j_{\mathrm{th}}(T_{1}) \exp\left(\frac{T-T_{1}}{T_{0}}\right)
\end{equation}
