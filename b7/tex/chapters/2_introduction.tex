\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Semiconductor lasers are an outstanding result of applied research in the 20th century.
The first semiconductor laser was built with $\ce{GaAs}$ in 1962. Decades of
continuous material and device optimization have made laser diodes standard
optoelectronic components, thus justifying this laboratory course. These advanced devices are used in modern technology,
primarily in fiber-optic communication and optical data storage.

A semiconductor laser uses radiative transitions of electrons between the
valence and conduction band to generate photons. This mechanism is discussed in
\cref{sec:optical_transitions}, with the help of preliminary semiconductor knowledge in
\cref{sec:semiconductor_foundations}. The laser features a zone in which
the light intensity increases if it is pumped sufficiently. This amplifying zone
is called the gain region and is examined in \cref{sec:absorption_coefficient_and_gain}. The light wave bounces back and forth in an optical cavity,
thereby constraining the available wavelengths. The proportion of light exiting the
device resembles a laser beam. These device characteristics are discussed in
\cref{sec:laser_diode} and were experimentally verified in \cref{sec:measurement_and_results}.

Semiconductor lasers can be made using a forward-biased PN Diode. As the effect is
caused by interband transitions, the emission wavelength is
determined primarily by the band gap of the material. Direct band gap semiconductors are suitable for laser
applications in different spectral ranges. Different material systems and their corresponding
wavelengths are displayed in \cref{fig:laser_materials}.
