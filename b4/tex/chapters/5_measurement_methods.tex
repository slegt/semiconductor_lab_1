\section{Measurement Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics{../assets/measurement_setup.pdf}
	\caption{Schematic of the measurement setup. \imcitetwo{script}}
	\label{fig:measurement_setup}
\end{figure}

The measurement setup is depicted in \cref{fig:measurement_setup}. A halogen lamp
generates a continuous spectrum, approximately that of a black body. The radiation
enters a monochromator, which selects a specified wavelength, and is then incident
on a chopper wheel that modulates it with a reference frequency. The radiation
reaches the photodiode, mounted in a cryostat, where it is converted to a
photocurrent. The c-meter applies a bias set through a digital-to-analog converter
and simultaneously reads the photocurrent, converting it to a voltage in the
manner of a transimpedance amplifier. This voltage is passed to a lock-in amplifier,
which is explained in \cref{subsec:lock_in}. Each measurement was integrated for \qty{3}{\second}.
Its output is digitized and collected by a PC\@. All experiments scanned a
photon energy from \qtyrange{600}{3000}{\milli\electronvolt} in steps of \qty{30}{\milli\electronvolt},
which corresponds to the wavelength range \qtyrange{413}{2066}{\nano\meter}.

\subsection{Lock-In Amplifier}
\label{subsec:lock_in}

As signals are obscured by noise, specialized measurement techniques are required
to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. If the carrier frequency is known, the signal
can be extracted from a noisy environment using a lock-in amplifier. Signals up
to a million times smaller than the noise components can be detected reliably
using this method. This section is based on \citeauthoryear{horowitz}.

A lock-in amplifier comprises a two-step arrangement. Some parameter of the source
signal is modulated to make it periodic, while the detected signal is
demodulated with respect to a reference signal. The modulation of the source signal
is realized using a chopper that rotates with the reference frequency. This
modulation moves the source signal above the $1 /f$ background noise.

\subsubsection{Circuit Design}
\label{subsec:circuit_design}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{../assets/lock_in.png}
	\caption{Circuit realizing a lock-in amplifier. \imcite{horowitz}}
	\label{fig:lock_in}
\end{figure}

The simplest circuit that realizes a lock-in amplifier is depicted in
\cref{fig:lock_in}. The input signal is passed through two linear amplifiers: an
inverting amplifier and a non-inverting amplifier of unity gain. The reference signal
is applied to a \ac{fet} switch that creates a mixing signal. The mixing signal is
then passed to a low-pass filter that extracts its time average, from which the
input signal voltage can be recovered. Noise at frequencies away from the reference frequency
is attenuated.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Modeling}
\label{subsec:lock_in_math}

Assume that an input signal $V_{\mathrm{s}}$ is a cosine signal:
\begin{equation}
	V_{\mathrm{s}}(t)=V_{0}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{s}}t+\theta) = \frac{V_{0}}{2}[\exp
	(\mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{s}}t)\exp(\mathrm{j}\theta)+\exp(-\mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{s}}
	t) \exp(-\mathrm{j}\theta)]. \label{eq:lock_in_signal}
\end{equation}
Here $V_{0}$ is the signal amplitude, $\omega_{\mathrm{s}}$ its angular frequency,
$\theta$ its phase relative to the reference and $\mathrm{j}$ the imaginary unit.
The reference signal $V_{\mathrm{r}}(t)$ is a square wave with angular frequency $\omega
_{\mathrm{r}}$. It can be modeled using a Fourier series:
\begin{equation}
	V_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = \operatorname{sign}[\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}t)] = \sum_{\substack{z \in \mathbb{Z} \\ \text{odd}}}
	c_{z}\exp(\mathrm{j}z \omega_{\mathrm{r}}t) \quad c_{z}=-\frac{2\mathrm{j}}{\pi
	z}. \label{eq:lock_in_reference}
\end{equation}
The $c_{z}$ are the Fourier coefficients of the square wave and $z$ is the
harmonic order, running over the odd integers. The output of the \ac{fet} switch is
mathematically equivalent to the multiplication of $V_{\mathrm{s}}(t)$ and $V_{\mathrm{r}}
(t)$. This results in a mixing signal
$V_{\mathrm{mix}}(t)=V_{\mathrm{s}}(t)V_{\mathrm{r}}(t)$:
\begin{equation}
	V_{\mathrm{mix}}(t)=\frac{V_{0}}{2}\sum_{z \text{ odd}}c_{z}[ \exp(\mathrm{j}\theta
	)\exp(\mathrm{j}(\underbrace{ \omega_{\mathrm{s}}+z \omega_{\mathrm{r}} }_{ \omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}
	})t) +\exp(-\mathrm{j}\theta) \exp(-\mathrm{j}(\underbrace{ \omega_{\mathrm{s}}-z
	\omega_{\mathrm{r}} }_{ \omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{-z} })t) ]. \label{eq:lock_in_mixing}
\end{equation}
The mixed signal is passed to a low-pass filter with time constant $\tau=RC$, set
by the filter resistance $R$ and capacitance $C$. The filter multiplies every
frequency component with its transfer function
$H(\omega) = (1+\mathrm{j}\omega \tau)^{-1}$. This results in the following output
voltage $V_{\mathrm{out}}(t)$:
\begin{align}
	V_{\mathrm{out}}(t) & =\frac{V_{0}}{2}\sum_{z \text{ odd}}c_{z}\left[ \frac{\exp(\mathrm{j}\theta)\exp(\mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}t)}{1 + \mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}\tau}+\frac{\exp(-\mathrm{j}\theta) \exp(-\mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{-z}t)}{1 - \mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{-z}\tau}\right] \\
	                    & =V_{0}\sum_{z \text{ odd}}\operatorname{Re}\left[ c_{z}\frac{\exp(\mathrm{j}\theta)\exp(\mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}t)}{1 + \mathrm{j}\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}\tau}\right] \quad \omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}=\omega_{\mathrm{s}}+z \omega_{\mathrm{r}}. \label{eq:lock_in_output}
\end{align}
The detected signal is obtained for $\omega_{\mathrm{s}}=\omega_{\mathrm{r}}$,
which is guaranteed here because the same chopper wheel generates both the
modulation and the reference. In that case $\omega_{\mathrm{mix}}^{z}=(1+z)\omega
_{\mathrm{r}}$, and since $z$ runs over the odd integers, $1+z$ is even and vanishes
only for $z=-1$. All remaining terms therefore lie at
$2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}, 4\omega_{\mathrm{r}}, \dots$ and are suppressed by the
low-pass filter. The time constant $\tau \gg T=2 \pi / \omega_{\mathrm{r}}$ also suppresses
the noise. Most noise components are not in the vicinity of
$z \omega_{\mathrm{r}}$, so they mix to high frequencies and are strongly attenuated
by the low-pass filter. Only the term $z=-1$ therefore survives, and the sum simplifies
to
\begin{equation}
	V_{\mathrm{out}}=-\frac{2V_{0}}{\pi}\sin(\theta). \label{eq:lock_in_result}
\end{equation}
The phase can be optimized manually to maximize the output voltage. From that, $V_{0}$
can be extracted directly or passed to an integration circuit to average the output
over a fixed time period (on the order of milliseconds to seconds).
