\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This report examines the photocurrent spectrum of five different photodiodes
(D1--D5), see
\cref{subsec:current_spectrum}. By recording a reference spectrum in \cref{subsec:reference_spectra},
the spectral responsivity and quantum efficiency of those photodiodes can be
extracted, see \cref{subsec:spectral_responsivity}. The response linearity with
respect to the optical power is examined in
\cref{subsec:linearity}. Although D3, an ordinary silicon photodiode, and D1, an
\ac{uv}-enhanced silicon photodiode, are based on silicon, their responsivity
spectra differ considerably. This difference is discussed in \cref{subsec:comparison}.
The responsivity of D4 is compared to a human eye response curve $V(\lambda )$, see \cref{subsec:eye}.
Finally, the modulation-frequency response of the photodiode is examined in
\cref{subsec:chopper}, where the reference frequency, and therefore the angular
speed of the chopper wheel, is varied at a fixed wavelength.

\subsection{Diode Current Spectrum}
\label{subsec:current_spectrum}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics{../plots/photocurrent.pdf}
	\caption{Photocurrent $I_{\mathrm{ph}}$ of the five photodiodes as a function
	of wavelength $\lambda$. Markers indicate the maximum of each spectrum at $\lambda
	_{\mathrm{max}}$.}
	\label{fig:photocurrent}
\end{figure}

Photocurrent spectra of five photodiodes were recorded and are depicted in \cref{fig:photocurrent}.
As all photodiodes are silicon-based, the band gap of
$E_{\mathrm{g}}=\qty{1.124}{\electronvolt}$
determines a minimal energy to excite electrons. This corresponds to a threshold
wavelength of $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=hc / E_{\mathrm{g}}=\qty{1103}{\nano\meter}$,
with $h$ the Planck constant and $c$ the vacuum speed of light. Above this
threshold wavelength the photocurrent drops by two to three orders of magnitude,
indicating that the signal vanished.

The photocurrent-spectra can be grouped into two categories, based on their line profile:
D3 and D5 peak at \qty{1000}{\nano \meter} while D1, D2 and D4 at around \qty{600}{\nano\meter}.
This difference is discussed in \cref{subsec:comparison}. The photocurrent significantly
drops for small wavelengths. This is a direct consequence of the decrease in
optical power of the halogen lamp, see \cref{subsec:reference_spectra}.

Using the average reference spectrum $P_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{avg}}$, the responsivity
$R_{\lambda}=I_{\mathrm{ph}}/ P_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{avg}}$ was calculated and
is depicted in \cref{fig:responsivity}. $R_{\lambda}$ does not depend on the power
of the optical source and is therefore a better device characteristic. The band gap
determines the highest wavelength threshold. The wavelengths
$\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}$ of maximum responsivity are marked in
\cref{fig:responsivity}; they lie close to the maxima of the raw photocurrent
spectra. The steep decrease towards low wavelengths is corrected, as it was caused
by the vanishing power of the source.

The external quantum efficiency spectrum is plotted in
\cref{fig:quantum_efficiency}. Evaluated at the responsivity maximum, D3 reaches
the highest quantum efficiency with \qty{37.14}{\percent} and D5 the lowest with
\qty{2.32}{\percent}. The characteristic values of all photodiodes are summarized
in \cref{tab:diodes}.

Direct comparisons between the absolute responsivities and quantum efficiencies are
not meaningful, given the high systematic error in the measurement setup. The
photodiode mount was readjusted after each photodiode installation. The detection
area generally differs from diode to diode and from that of the reference detector.
Therefore, the responsivity and external quantum efficiency are only a rough
estimate.

\subsection{Photodiode Detector Linearity}
\label{subsec:linearity}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics{../plots/linearity.pdf}
	\caption{Relative photocurrent of diodes 4 and 5 as a function of the relative
	incident photon flux, together with power-law fits of slope $m$.}
	\label{fig:linearity}
\end{figure}

The photocurrents of D4 and D5 were measured at a fixed wavelength while the optical
power was varied using neutral density filters, depicted in \cref{fig:linearity}.
At a fixed wavelength the incident photon flux is proportional to the optical power,
so that \cref{eq:photocurrent} suggests
$I_{\mathrm{ph}}\propto P_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and therefore
\begin{equation}
	\frac{I}{I_{0}}=\frac{P}{P_{0}}
	,
\end{equation}
with $I$ the photocurrent and $P$ the optical power behind the filter stack, and
$I_{0}$ and $P_{0}$ the values measured without any filter. A straight line
$f(x)=mx+n$ fitted to the doubly logarithmic data corresponds to the power law
$I/I_{0}=\exp(n)(P/P_{0})^{m}$, so that its slope $m$ is the exponent of the
original data, its intercept $n$ fixes the prefactor $\exp(n)$, and $m=1$
describes a linear response.

D4 shows a nearly linear scaling with $m=\num{0.92(1)}$. However, D5 shows two
regimes, with an unphysical break at \num{2e-2}. While the second regime features a
nearly linear scaling with $m=\num{0.94(9)}$, the first regime only scales as
$m=\num{0.62(2)}$.

\subsection{Comparison between Si- and \acs{uv}-Enhanced Si-Photodiodes}
\label{subsec:comparison}

Both the Si-Photodiode D3 and the \ac{uv}-enhanced Si-Photodiode D1 are
silicon-based and therefore share the threshold wavelength of \qty{1103}{\nano\meter}.
However, their line profiles differ considerably. D1 has a maximum responsivity at
\qty{558}{\nano\meter}, while D3 features a maximum responsivity at \qty{898}{\nano\meter}.

As the measurement does not enter the \ac{uv}-regime (\qtyrange{250}{400}{\nano\meter}),
no statements can be concluded about their responsivity in this region. It is
expected that D1 features a considerably higher responsivity.

For an ordinary silicon photodiode, infrared radiation penetrates deeply into
the material due to silicon's reduced absorption coefficient: The generation of electron-hole
pairs takes place in the whole \ac{scr}\@. However \ac{uv} radiation does not penetrate deep
into the material due to a higher absorption coefficient. A large proportion of electron-hole
pair generation happens in the inversion layer. These pairs have a high chance
to recombine at the surface and effectively do not contribute to the
photocurrent. The Schottky photodiode therefore features a responsivity falloff in
the \ac{uv}-regime attributed to the presence of an inversion layer in the depletion region.

\ac{uv}-enhanced Si-photodiodes may feature higher doping concentrations $N_{\mathrm{D}}$.
This implies a shrinking inversion layer width as well as a shrinking \ac{scr}
width as both scale as $\propto 1 / \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{D}}}$~\autocite{korde}.
\ac{uv} radiation is mostly absorbed in the \ac{scr} and electron-hole
excitation results in a current. However the \ac{scr} is too narrow for infrared radiation,
as a large proportion passes through it. This optimization leads to a responsivity
loss in the near infrared regime.

Differences in the surface coating may additionally influence the responsivity.
The line profiles suggest that D5 is an ordinary photodiode, whereas D2 and D4 are
\ac{uv}-enhanced photodiodes.

\subsection{Human Eye Response}
\label{subsec:eye}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics{../plots/human_eye.pdf}
	\caption{Normalized spectral responsivity of diode 4 compared to the photopic
	and scotopic response curves $V(\lambda)$ of the human eye.}
	\label{fig:human_eye}
\end{figure}

This section is based on \citeauthoryear{paschotta}. Human eye vision is based
on four types of photoreceptors: rods as well as three types of cones. Depending
on the incoming luminance, different photoreceptor signals are evaluated to determine
the brightness. Photopic vision operates under bright-light conditions, whereas
scotopic vision operates under low-light conditions. Each
photoreceptor uses a different photopigment that changes chemically under light
absorption.

Scotopic vision uses rods, whose rhodopsin photopigment is more light sensitive than
the cone pigments. Photopic vision uses cones, each type featuring its own photopigment
with slightly different absorption maxima.

The $V(\lambda)$ curves for scotopic and photopic vision, depicted together with
the normalized responsivity of D4 in \cref{fig:human_eye}, are experimentally determined.
It is not a physical property of a photoreceptor, but the outcome of a psychological
exeriment averaged over a panel of human observers \autocite{grundmann}. Each observer
equalizes the brightness of a monochromatic light with $\lambda_{0}= \qty{555}{\nano\meter}$
to that of a light source with wavelength $\lambda$ with the same radiation power
using flicker photometry. The experiment was conducted under bright-light and
under low-light conditions and the relative radiative power is used to construct
$V(\lambda)$.

The three curves peak at comparable wavelengths: at \qty{505}{\nano\meter} for
scotopic vision, at \qty{555}{\nano\meter} for photopic vision and at
\qty{582}{\nano\meter} for D4. However, the peak width of D4 is considerably
larger than that of either $V(\lambda)$ curve.
Without any correction filter, D4 cannot be used as a photometric diode.

\subsection{Chopper Frequency Dependence}
\label{subsec:chopper}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics{../plots/chopper.pdf}
	\caption{Photocurrent $I_{\mathrm{ph}}$ as a function of the reference
	frequency $f$. Blue, green and orange dots indicate an integration time of
	\qty{1}{\second}, \qty{3}{\second} and \qty{10}{\second} respectively.}
	\label{fig:chopper}
\end{figure}

A dependence of the photocurrent on the modulation frequency is expected. In order
to observe this effect, the photocurrent was measured at a fixed wavelength for
different reference frequencies, resulting in different chopper angular velocities.
The result is depicted in \cref{fig:chopper}. No change in photocurrent is observed
for high frequencies.

However, a sharp falloff at low frequencies is observed. A possible explanation
of this behaviour is a coupling capacitor that connects two stages acting as a
high-pass filter. Using the fit function
\begin{equation}
	I_{\mathrm{ph}}(f)=\frac{I_{\mathrm{sat}}f}{\sqrt{ f^{2}+f_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}}
\end{equation}
with the saturation current $I_{\mathrm{sat}}$ and the cutoff frequency
$f_{\mathrm{c}}$, the fit yields $f_{\mathrm{c}}=\qty{0.77(2)}{\hertz}$ and
$I_{\mathrm{sat}}=\qty{125.0(2)}{\nano\ampere}$.
