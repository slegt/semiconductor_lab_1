\section{Semiconductor Foundations}
\label{sec:theory_basics}

To analyze the Schottky diode analytically, the essential prerequisites from
semiconductor physics are presented in the following sections. These are taken from
\citeauthoryear{grundmann}.

Expressions for the current density are used together with the electron-hole
continuity equations to find the photocurrent dependence on the generation rate. The
generation rate itself can be described with the photon continuity equation and the
Lambert-Beer law.

\subsection{Current Density}
\label{sec:current_density}

In solid-state physics, a current density can only be established if the electronic
bands are neither completely empty nor completely filled. Especially in
semiconductor physics, electrons in the almost-empty conduction band as well as holes
in the almost-filled valence band contribute to the current density. Let
$j_{\mathrm{n}}$ be the electron current density and $j_{\mathrm{p}}$ be the
hole current density. The total current density can be calculated as $j_{\mathrm{tot}}
= j_{\mathrm{n}}(z) + j_{\mathrm{p}}(z)$. In a device with constant cross
section $A$, the total current is obtained as $I=A j_{\mathrm{tot}}$.

Currents originate either from an electric field (drift current) or from a concentration
gradient (diffusion current). For nondegenerate semiconductors, this results in:
\begin{align}
	j_{\mathrm{p}} & = e \mu_{\mathrm{p}}p E - e D_{\mathrm{p}}\partial_{z}p, \label{eq:j_p}   \\
	j_{\mathrm{n}} & = -e \mu_{\mathrm{n}}n E - e D_{\mathrm{n}}\partial_{z}n. \label{eq:j_n}
\end{align}
In those equations, $e$ is the elementary charge, $n / p$ is the charge carrier
concentration for electrons / holes, $\mu_{\mathrm{n/p}}$ is the mobility of the respective
charge carrier, $E$ is the magnitude of the electric field and $D_{\mathrm{n/p}}$ is
the diffusion coefficient of the respective charge carrier.

\subsection{Electron-Hole Continuity Equation}
\label{sec:continuity}

In an infinitesimally small volume at position $\mathbf{r}$, the temporal change
of electron and hole density is determined by generation rates, summarized in $G_{\mathrm{n/p}}
(\mathbf{r})$ for electrons / holes, recombination rates, summarized in $U_{\mathrm{n/p}}
(\mathbf{r})$ for electrons / holes and the current densities $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{n/p}}$
that flow through the volume's surface. This results in the continuity equations
below:
\begin{align}
	\partial_{t}n & = G_{\mathrm{n}}- U_{\mathrm{n}}+ \frac{1}{e}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{n}}, \\
	\partial_{t}p & = G_{\mathrm{p}}- U_{\mathrm{p}}- \frac{1}{e}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{p}}.
\end{align}
In most two-terminal devices, characteristics only depend on one direction $(z)$,
while being independent in their cross section. This reduces the complexity to a
one-dimensional problem:
\begin{align}
	\partial_{t}n(z) & = G_{\mathrm{n}}(z) - U_{\mathrm{n}}(z) + \frac{1}{e}\partial_{z}j_{\mathrm{n}}(z), \label{eq:continuity_n} \\
	\partial_{t}p(z) & = G_{\mathrm{p}}(z) - U_{\mathrm{p}}(z) - \frac{1}{e}\partial_{z}j_{\mathrm{p}}(z). \label{eq:continuity_p}
\end{align}
As electron-hole pairs are usually generated or recombined in pairs, their
generation and recombination rates are identical: $G=G_{\mathrm{n}}=G_{\mathrm{p}}$
and $U=U_{\mathrm{n}}=U_{\mathrm{p}}$.

\subsection{Photon Continuity Equation}
\label{sec:photon_continuity}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{../assets/photon_continuity.png}
	\caption{Photon balance of a slab of width $\delta$: photons entering the slab are
	either absorbed inside it or leave it on the opposite side.}
	\label{fig:photon_continuity}
\end{figure}

Let a beam of light enter the material and let the $z$-direction be parallel to the
beam direction. Divide the material into thin slices of width $\delta$ with
cross section $A$. Let $\Phi(z)$ denote the photon flux, $n_{\mathrm{ph}}(z)$
the photon density and $U_{\mathrm{ph}}$ the photon absorption rate per unit
volume in a two-terminal device. The photon balance depicted in \cref{fig:photon_continuity} yields:
\begin{equation}
	\underbrace{ \partial_{t}(n_{\mathrm{ph}}(z,t)A\delta) }_{ \text{change of
	photons} }= \underbrace{ \Phi(z)A }_{ \substack{\text{incoming} \\ \text{photons}}
	}- \underbrace{ \Phi(z+\delta)A }_{ \substack{\text{outgoing} \\ \text{photons}}
	}- \underbrace{ U_{\mathrm{ph}}A\delta }_{ \substack{\text{photon} \\ \text{sink}}
	}.
\end{equation}
Dividing by $\delta A$ and taking the limit $\delta \to 0$ yields the photon
continuity equation:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_{t}n_{\mathrm{ph}}= - \frac{\mathrm{d} \Phi}{\mathrm{d}z}-U_{\mathrm{ph}}
	.
\end{equation}
In steady state $\partial_{t}n_{\mathrm{ph}}= 0$, resulting in:
\begin{equation}
	U_{\mathrm{ph}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d} \Phi}{\mathrm{d}z}. \label{eq:photon_continuity_steady}
\end{equation}
The photon absorption rate is therefore equal to the negative derivative of the photon
flux.

\subsection{Lambert-Beer Law}
\label{sec:lambert_beer}

Assume an identical setup as in \cref{sec:photon_continuity}. The photon flux that
is absorbed in the slice is proportional to the incoming flux and the slice width
with proportionality constant $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
	\Phi(z) - \Phi(z+\delta) = \alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi(z) \delta.
\end{equation}
Taking the limit $\delta \to 0$ yields the following differential equation:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}=-\alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi(z).
\end{equation}
The quantity $\alpha(h\nu, z)$ is the absorption coefficient, which depends on
the photon energy $h\nu$ (with $h$ being the Planck constant and $\nu$ the photon
frequency) and on the position $z$. A general solution for $z$-dependent $\alpha$
can be constructed using a separation of variables ansatz:
\begin{align}
	\frac{\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}              & = \exp\left( -\int_{0}^{z}\alpha(h \nu, z') \, \mathrm{d}z' \right),                                                 \\
	\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}z} & = -\alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi_{0}\exp\left( -\int_{0}^{z}\alpha(h\nu, z') \, \mathrm{d}z' \right). \label{eq:flux_general}
\end{align}
Here, $\Phi_{0}=\Phi(z=0)$ is the incident flux entering the material. If
$\alpha$ is independent of $z$, this reduces to the Lambert-Beer law:
\begin{equation}
	\Phi(z) = \Phi_{0}\exp(-\alpha(h\nu) z). \label{eq:lambert_beer}
\end{equation}
