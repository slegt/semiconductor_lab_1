\section{Semiconductor Photodetector Theory}

\subsection{Semiconductor Current Density}

Let $j_{\mathrm{n}}$ be the \textbf{electron current density} and $j_{\mathrm{p}}$
be the \textbf{hole current density}. The total current density can be calculated
as $j_{\mathrm{tot}}= j_{\mathrm{n}}(z) + j_{\mathrm{p}}(z)$. In a device with
constant cross section $A$, the total current is obtained as $I=A j_{\mathrm{tot}}$.

In semiconductor physics, currents originate either from an electric field (\emph{drift
current}) or from a concentration gradient (\emph{diffusion current}). For \emph{nondegenerate}
semiconductors, this results in:

\[
	\begin{align}
		j_{\mathrm{p}}=e \mu_{\mathrm{p}}p E-eD_{\mathrm{p}}\partial_{z}p   \\
		j_{\mathrm{n}}=-e \mu_{\mathrm{n}}n E -eD_{\mathrm{n}}\partial_{z}n
	\end{align}
\]
Those equations feature

\begin{itemize}
	\item Elementary charge $e$

	\item Charge Carrier Concentration $n / p$

	\item Mobility of the respective charge carrier $\mu_{\mathrm{n/p}}$

	\item Electric field $E$

	\item The \emph{Einstein Coefficient} $D= kT \mu/q$, with the Boltzmann constant
		$k$, the temperature $T$ and the charge $q$ of the respective carrier
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Electron-Hole Continuity Equation}

In an infinitesimally small volume at $\mathbf{r}$, the temporal change of electron
and hole density is determined by \emph{generation rates}, summarized in $G_{\mathrm{n/p}}
(\mathbf{r})$ for electrons / holes, \emph{recombination rates}, summarized in
$U_{\mathrm{n/p}}$ for electrons / holes and the Semiconductor Current
Density|current densities $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{n/p}}$ that flow through the
volume's surface. The elementary charge is denoted by $e$.

\[
	\begin{align}
		\partial_{t}n & = G_{\mathrm{n}}- U_{\mathrm{n}}+ \frac{1}{e}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{n}} \\
		\partial_{t}p & = G_{\mathrm{p}}- U_{\mathrm{p}}- \frac{1}{e}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{p}}
	\end{align}
\]
In most two-terminal devices, characteristics only depend on one direction $(z)$,
while being independent in its cross section. This reduces the complexity as:

\[
	\begin{align}
		\partial_{t}n(z) & = G_{\mathrm{n}}(z) - U_{\mathrm{n}}(z) + \frac{1}{e}\partial_{z}j_{\mathrm{n}}(z) \\
		\partial_{t}p(z) & = G_{\mathrm{p}}(z) - U_{\mathrm{p}}(z) - \frac{1}{e}\partial_{z}j_{\mathrm{p}}(z)
	\end{align}
\]

\subsection{Photon Continuity Equation}

Let $\Phi(z)$ denote the \emph{photon flux}, $n_{\mathrm{ph}}(z)$ the \emph{photon
density} and $U_{\mathrm{ph}}$ the \emph{photon absorption rate} per unit volume
in a two-terminal device. For a small slab $[z, z+\delta]$ of cross section $A$,
the following statement is true:

\[
	\underbrace{ \partial_{t}(n_{\mathrm{ph}}(z,t)A\delta) }_{ \text{change of
	photons} }=\underbrace{ \Phi(z)A }_{ \substack{\text{incoming} \\ \text{photons}}
	}-\underbrace{ \Phi(z+\delta)A }_{ \substack{\text{outgoing} \\ \text{photons}}
	}-\underbrace{ U_{\mathrm{ph}}A\delta }_{ \substack{\text{photon} \\ \text{sink}}
	}
\]
Dividing by $\delta A$ and $\delta \to 0$ yields the \textbf{photon continuity
equation}:

\[
	\partial_{t}n_{\mathrm{ph}}=- \frac{\mathrm{d} \Phi}{\mathrm{d}z}-U_{\mathrm{ph}}
\]
In \emph{steady state} $\partial_{t}n_{\mathrm{ph}}= 0$, resulting in:

\[
	U_{\mathrm{ph}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d} \Phi}{\mathrm{d}z}
\]

\subsection{Lambert-Beer Law}

Let a beam of light enter a material and let the $z$ direction be parallel to the
beam direction. Divide the material into thin slices of width $\delta$. The \emph{radiant
flux} that is absorbed in the slice is proportional to the incoming flux and the
slice width with proportionality constant $\alpha$:

\[
	\Phi(z) - \Phi(z+\delta) = \alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi(z) \delta
\]
Approaching $\delta \to 0$ yields the following differential equation:

\[
	\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}=-\alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi(z)
\]
The quantity $\alpha(h\nu, z)$ is the \emph{absorption coefficient}, which
depends on the photon energy $h\nu$ and on the position $z$. A general solution for
$z$-dependent $\alpha$ can be constructed using the Separation of Variables ansatz:

\[
	\begin{align}
		\frac{\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}              & =\exp\left( -\int_{0}^{z}\alpha(h \nu, z') \, \mathrm{d}z' \right)                         \\
		\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}z} & =-\alpha(h\nu, z) \Phi_{0}\exp\left( -\int_{0}^{z}\alpha(h\nu, z') \, \mathrm{d}z' \right)
	\end{align}
\]
Here, $\Phi_{0}=\Phi(z=0)$ is the incident flux entering the material. If $\alpha$
is independent of $z$, this reduces to the well-known \textbf{Lambert-Beer law}:

\[
	\Phi(z) = \Phi_{0}\exp(-\alpha(h\nu) z)
\]
